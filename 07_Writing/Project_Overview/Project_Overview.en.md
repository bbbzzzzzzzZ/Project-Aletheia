# Project Aletheia: A Ten-Minute Overview

> This is a public-facing overview. It answers four questions: why the project exists, what it currently holds, how its models connect, and what remains unsettled. It is not a completed social blueprint or an implementable institutional plan.

## 1. Why study this?

Project Aletheia began from ordinary but persistent questions.

Why does help sometimes fail to produce equality, instead fixing the helper as the person who is “supposed” to do the work? Why are care, cleaning, service, repair, and maintenance indispensable, yet the people who perform them are often treated as lower in value? Why do occupation, income, ability, and contribution - originally descriptions of certain actions - so often become judgments about the worth of a person?

These questions do not belong only to labor. In education, a child may be defined too early by a score or career track. In organizations, those who control information, resources, or expertise may gain power beyond the task. In public life, the phrase “everyone needs this” may turn too quickly into a task that some particular person cannot refuse.

Aletheia therefore does not begin by asking which institution should be built. It first asks: How do persons want to live? How do persons relate to one another? When needs become connected, how can cooperation avoid turning one person into the owner of another? What should society support, and where should institutions stop?

The project currently follows the order: person -> relation -> society -> institution. A person does not exist in order to fill a social function. Society exists because persons exist. Institutions are tools for maintaining and correcting shared life; they may create conditions, coordinate conflict, and organize public matters, but they should not prescribe a single correct life for everyone.

This is not an individualist fantasy of isolated persons. Freedom depends on food, care, knowledge, infrastructure, and invisible labor. Mutual dependence requires common responsibility. But common responsibility without limits can become unlimited control over time, body, and life direction. Aletheia studies how basic guarantees, personal freedom, and limited common responsibility can coexist.

## 2. The seven core propositions

### 1. The person comes before identity and function

Institutions face persons before they face workers, roles, abilities, or contribution units. An occupation says what someone is doing now; it does not exhaust their possibility. Ability may show fitness for a task; it does not determine human rank. Contribution may be recorded and appreciated; it cannot be converted into superior personhood.

### 2. A person is the first speaker of their own need, but need is not command

A person is closest to their own experience, situation, and life purpose. They should have the first opportunity to say what they need. Others may help interpret facts, consequences, and effects on others, but they should not take over the person’s life purpose.

At the same time, expressing a need does not mean that every need must be satisfied, nor that the speaker may command others or occupy common resources. Expression makes a question visible. Public responsibility requires additional judgment.

### 3. First ask whose need it is; then ask who did what

A need appearing in one person does not always belong only to that person. A purely private preference is first the person’s own need. Others may accept or refuse. By contrast, basic medical care is not merely the private trouble of the current patient. Doctors, nurses, children, elderly persons, and healthy persons all inhabit vulnerability to illness and injury. A medical system is therefore a public need, revealed in a particular case.

Need attribution is the starting point of responsibility. It is not the same as personal blame, direct execution, or authority over resources.

### 4. Basic life is not exchanged for performance, and common responsibility must be limited and fair

Basic survival and basic rights should not disappear because someone has not contributed, has refused a task, or has failed to fulfill a mutual responsibility. Basic guarantees are not rewards for good behavior or instruments for obedience.

Still, common life cannot rely forever on invisible others. Public needs must be organized, financed, maintained, and supervised. Persons with relevant real capacity may have limited and fair responsibilities, but “everyone needs this” does not automatically mean “this person must do it.”

### 5. Help is not charity, and receiving help is not a debt of personhood

Illness, aging, disability, incapacity, and care needs are not special defects of a lower class of people. They are conditions into which anyone may enter.

Real labor and care should be recognized. But basic care cannot be settled by exact personal merit accounting. Otherwise, mutual aid becomes domination: helpers demand obedience, and recipients must repeatedly prove that they deserve to live.

### 6. Ability, resources, or influence do not automatically authorize one person to decide for another

Some qualifications belong to everyone: basic life, expression, dissent, and the right to seek help. Other forms of control may arise from access to resources, information, opportunities, or complaint channels. Such factual power matters even without a formal title.

For a particular task, a community may temporarily authorize a person or institution to act. That authority must remain limited to the task, open to review, and returned when conditions change.

### 7. Deciding what to do, who bears the task, and who may act are different steps; institutions must be revisable

A common decision only says what is currently to be done. It does not automatically assign a person, nor does it naturally give the executor broader control. Task assignment, qualification, authority, oversight, and correction must be separately explained.

Institutions are not final at birth. Rules may omit people, underestimate cost, or allow hidden control. A good institution must be able to revise, pause, compensate, or end when evidence, burden, dissent, and harm show that it is wrong.

## 3. How the models connect

The seven propositions set boundaries. The project’s models provide different ways to examine a case. They are not a pipeline that automatically turns human problems into institutional tasks.

The first layer is the person’s life and relationships. Who is experiencing what? Do they need to be heard, to understand, to act privately, to change a relation, or to invite others into a specific cooperation? Many matters can remain here. Listening, withdrawal, refusal, private help, and voluntary cooperation are complete responses.

Only when a matter separately shows common impact, rights protection, public resources, recurring risk, or structured coordination does it become a candidate for institutional treatment.

When a matter does require coordination, the project asks:

- Who is affected, and who may be missing?
- What resources are required, and who bears scarcity or opportunity cost?
- Which parts of the case are private needs, organizational needs, role-system needs, or public needs?
- Who expresses, interprets, organizes, decides, executes, supervises, and repairs?
- Who is formally authorized, and who in fact controls access, records, metrics, or appeal?
- What happens when the result harms someone or reveals that the original interpretation was wrong?

These questions correct one another. Scarcity may require a smaller goal. A newly identified affected party may require reopening the decision. Workers may report that conditions are unsafe. Harm may require returning from institutional design to lived experience.

## 4. What remains unsettled

Aletheia has not yet solved:

- the minimum concrete content of basic guarantees across regions;
- how limited public responsibility should be distributed;
- how to prevent contribution records from becoming hidden status hierarchies;
- how to finance public operation without expanding domination;
- how to distinguish expertise from authority over life purposes;
- how to design exit, appeal, correction, and compensation in real institutions;
- how to test the framework against existing systems without harming real persons.

The project has begun preparing low-risk validation materials, such as a baseline case of a forty-year-old worker in Shanghai transitioning into elder care work. That case does not prove Aletheia. It is a way to compare actual transition costs, training costs, wage uncertainty, insurance continuity, certification incentives, and recruitment conditions.

High-risk domains, such as reproduction, child welfare, and family formation, should remain at the level of legal, ethical, and anonymous scenario review until safeguards are much stronger.

## 5. How to read the project

Read Aletheia as a research framework, not as a manifesto waiting to be enforced. Its central question is where institutions should intervene and where they must stop.
