## 中文摘要

本文提出 Project Aletheia 的哲学基础与制度设计草案。它的核心问题不是“应当由谁管理社会”，而是：如果人不是制度、组织、市场或他人的材料，制度为什么仍然必要，又应当在哪里停止。本文区分四类命题：价值判断、事实判断、经验假设和制度推论。Aletheia 的价值判断是：人是目的，是会为了生存而合作、为了需求而负责、并拥有自主性的自由个体。它的事实判断是：人的需求会随时间和处境变化，人也并不总能立即清楚、正确或可实现地理解自己的需求。它的经验假设是：若制度只保障共同生存底线，而不统一个体需求和人生目的，个体更可能在不被胁迫的条件下自愿合作。由此推出的制度方向是：共同体应保障生命安全和基本自由，公共责任应来自共同需求而不是来自对个体的总体管理，权力必须受授权、期限、退出、申诉和纠错约束。

本文进一步提出五层需求优先序：生命安全需求、基本自由与独立发展需求、公共运行需求、外部协作型发展需求、消费需求。该结构用于区分“基本保障”“自主空间”“公共协作”和“私人或市场偏好”，避免把公共需求私人化，也避免把私人欲望公共化。本文还讨论弱者的选择权、强者能力与领导力的制度边界、劳动主体的承认、效率提升的分配，以及创新如何从私人便利、市场需求或协作发展回流为公共需求。本文不主张 Aletheia 已被实证证明，也不主张任何具体机制已经可以实施；它只提供一组可检验、可修正的哲学与制度设计框架。

关键词：人的主体性；公共责任；制度边界；需求归属；权力授权；劳动承认；制度设计。

## Abstract

This working paper presents Project Aletheia as a philosophical framework and institutional design sketch. Its central question is not who should manage society, but why institutions are needed at all if human beings are not materials for institutions, organizations, markets, or other persons. The paper separates four kinds of claims: value judgments, descriptive judgments, empirical hypotheses, and institutional inferences. Aletheia begins with the value judgment that a person is an end: a free and self-directed being who cooperates for survival and becomes responsible through needs, commitments, actions, and effects. It also begins from the descriptive judgment that human needs are fluid, context-dependent, and often unclear even to the person who has them. The framework therefore rejects any immediate transition from expressed need to authority over others, while also rejecting the privatization of common vulnerability.

The paper develops a five-layer priority structure of needs: life and safety needs; basic freedom and independent development needs; public operation needs; externally collaborative development needs; and consumption needs. This structure is intended to distinguish survival guarantees, autonomous space, public coordination, resource-dependent development, and private or market preferences. Public responsibility arises when a need belongs to the community as a condition of shared life, not when an individual happens to suffer or speak first. Authority, in turn, must be limited by authorization, scope, duration, participation, exit, appeal, and correction. The paper also sketches how the framework handles weaker agents, stronger agents, labor recognition, efficiency gains, and the transformation of innovations into public needs. It does not claim that Aletheia has been empirically validated or that any concrete institutional mechanism is ready for implementation. Its contribution is a testable and revisable grammar for asking when institutions should intervene, when they should stop, and how responsibility and power can be prevented from swallowing the person they are meant to serve.

Keywords: human subjectivity; public responsibility; institutional boundaries; needs; authority; labor recognition; political philosophy; institutional design.

## 1. Introduction: the question before institutional design

Modern institutional debate often begins too late. It begins after society has already been imagined as a system to be administered, after persons have already been classified as workers, patients, students, consumers, citizens, dependents, or risks, and after the basic question has quietly disappeared: what is the institution for?

Project Aletheia begins from that missing question. It asks what kind of being a person is, what it means for persons to live together, and under what conditions an institution may legitimately enter into that life. The project does not begin by proposing a completed constitution, welfare regime, labor system, or economic plan. It begins by asking how need, responsibility, and power can remain connected without allowing any one of them to dominate the others.

The name “Aletheia” is used in the sense of disclosure or unconcealment. The project is not an attempt to tell people how they ought to live. It is an attempt to reveal the layers that are often hidden inside ordinary institutional judgment: Who is experiencing a need? Who is interpreting it? Who claims it is public? Who bears its cost? Who gains authority by naming it? Who can refuse? Who can leave? Who can correct the result when the original decision was wrong?

The central worry is simple. Institutions are created to help human beings live together, but institutions can also invert their purpose. They can begin to treat human beings as material for institutional survival, organizational efficiency, national strength, market productivity, moral order, or someone else’s private desire. A school may treat a child as a future worker rather than as a person learning to live. A labor market may treat a person’s time as if it naturally belongs to the job. A welfare system may treat survival as a reward for compliance. A family, firm, party, profession, or expert class may claim the power to define what another person really needs. In each case, the institution no longer serves human life; human life is reorganized to serve the institution.

Aletheia’s guiding question is therefore:

> How can a community protect the conditions of shared survival while refusing to make survival itself conditional on being managed, bound, or coerced?

This paper offers a compressed statement of the framework as of Project Aletheia v0.4.0. It is a working paper and design sketch. It does not claim empirical validation, public authorization, legal status, or institutional readiness. Its aim is to make the philosophical structure clear enough to be criticized.

## 2. Four kinds of claims

Aletheia separates four kinds of claims that are often mixed together in political and institutional writing: value judgments, descriptive judgments, empirical hypotheses, and institutional inferences. The separation matters because each kind of claim fails in a different way and needs a different form of criticism.

### 2.1 Value judgment

The value judgment is that the human person is an end, not a material to be used by institutions, organizations, markets, or other persons. A person is a being who cooperates for survival, becomes responsible through needs and effects, and possesses a sphere of self-direction that cannot be absorbed into public administration.

This does not mean that persons are isolated atoms. Aletheia is not a theory of pure private independence. Human beings need one another. They are born dependent, become capable through care, language, learning, and social inheritance, and remain vulnerable to illness, age, accident, violence, and environmental collapse. But dependence does not imply ownership. Need does not imply domination. Care does not imply the right to define another person’s life. The fact that we must live together does not mean that anyone may turn another person’s existence into an instrument.

The negative form of the same judgment is this: no one should acquire, for merely private reasons, the authority to use, bind, manage, or sacrifice another person’s existence. A community may protect common survival conditions. It may coordinate shared tasks. It may restrain harm. But it may not transform survival itself into a reward for obedience to an individual, a group, an office, or an abstract goal.

### 2.2 Descriptive judgment

The descriptive judgment is that human needs are fluid. They change with time, environment, body, relationships, knowledge, and available resources. A person does not always know what they need. Even when a person can name a need, they may not know whether the interpretation is reasonable, whether the proposed solution is possible, or whether the demand imposes unacceptable costs on others.

This is not a paternalistic claim. It does not say that experts, institutions, or majorities know a person’s needs better than the person does. It says that the experience of need, the interpretation of need, the plan for satisfying need, and the public justification of need are not the same act. A person may feel pain before understanding its cause. A community may identify a common danger before agreeing on a solution. A private preference may become more intense without becoming a public claim. A public condition may appear through one person’s suffering even though the need belongs to everyone.

The descriptive judgment therefore blocks two opposite errors. First, it blocks the authoritarian error of taking uncertainty as permission for others to define a person from above. Second, it blocks the voluntarist error of treating the first expression of a need as if it automatically generated authority over other people and public resources.

### 2.3 Empirical hypothesis

Aletheia’s central empirical hypothesis is modest but important:

> If institutions guarantee the minimum conditions of shared survival without unifying individual needs, purposes, or forms of life, persons are more likely to cooperate voluntarily, conflicts may be reduced, and institutional stability may improve.

This is not yet a proven social-scientific law. It is a hypothesis that must be tested, narrowed, revised, or rejected. It requires evidence about who gains choice, who bears cost, who is excluded, whether coercion is actually reduced, whether cooperation is genuinely voluntary, and whether errors can be exited and repaired.

The hypothesis should also be compared with competing explanations. A society with broader survival guarantees may become more cooperative because fear is reduced, but it might also face coordination costs, free-riding problems, bureaucratic expansion, new forms of dependency, or capture by organized interests. Aletheia does not deny these risks. It treats them as reasons to design institutional boundaries and failure tests rather than as reasons to leave survival at the mercy of market price, family hierarchy, or administrative command.

### 2.4 Institutional inference

The institutional inference is that a justifiable institution should be organized around five constraints:

1. shared survival as a floor;
2. individual autonomy as a protected space;
3. voluntary cooperation and limited public responsibility as ordinary mechanisms;
4. rights boundaries and procedural justice as constraints;
5. revisability as protection against error.

This inference does not directly produce a full constitution, task-allocation system, budget, court procedure, or labor plan. It provides a grammar for judging such proposals. A proposal must explain which need it addresses, whose need it is, why it is public if it claims public force, who is affected, what authority is requested, how long that authority lasts, what alternatives exist, how costs are distributed, how refusal works, and how mistakes are corrected.

## 3. First principles and forbidden crossings

Aletheia v0.4.0 keeps a frozen “First Principles v1.0” layer. The freeze is not a declaration that the principles have been empirically validated or publicly authorized. It is a project-internal decision that downstream mechanisms may not silently rewrite the normative starting point.

The first principles can be summarized as follows.

First, personhood precedes function. A person is not exhausted by job, family role, productivity, contribution, status, diagnosis, or capacity. Second, the person is the first speaker of their own need, although not the only judge of every fact or public implication. Third, need creates the right to express, interpret, request, and act, but it does not by itself create authority over others. Fourth, responsibility follows the subject to whom a need belongs, not simply the person who first expresses it. Fifth, ability is a condition of task qualification, not a general title to rule others or a permanent debt to serve.

The principles also separate basic rights, factual power, and authorized authority. A person may have a basic right regardless of whether they control resources. A person may exercise factual power without formal office. A person may receive limited authority for a task without owning the domain in which the task occurs. Confusing these three categories is one of the main sources of institutional domination.

Personal time is presumed to belong to the person. Basic survival guarantees are not conditional on labor performance. Shared vulnerability must not become a hierarchy of persons. Public needs, once properly recognized, are borne by the public as a community rather than thrown back onto the person through whom the need appears. Finally, institutions must not define happiness for others.

The forbidden crossings are equally important. No mechanism may use basic survival as a hostage for obedience. No office may convert temporary authority into permanent ownership. No expert, majority, family member, employer, or algorithm may replace the person as the author of their life. No contribution record may become a measure of human worth. No emergency may become a normal method of rule. No public good may erase the affected person, the worker who performs the task, or the dissenting party who bears the cost.

These are not yet institutional details. They are constraints on institutional imagination.

## 4. The five-layer structure of needs

Aletheia distinguishes five layers of need. The layers are not a complete psychology of desire, nor a fixed ranking of all goods. They are a priority structure for deciding when public responsibility, protected freedom, coordination, support, or refusal is appropriate.

### 4.1 Life and safety needs

The first layer includes survival floors: food, water, basic shelter, urgent medical care, basic bodily safety, and minimal access required to preserve life. These needs receive the highest priority. They are guaranteed without becoming a personal debt of obedience. A person does not have to prove usefulness before receiving the conditions necessary to remain alive.

This layer is intentionally minimal. It is not the whole good life. It prevents the most basic form of domination: making a person’s continued existence depend on submitting to another’s will.

### 4.2 Basic freedom and independent development needs

The second layer protects private freedom, thought, learning, expression, ordinary creation, and independent development that can occur without consuming scarce public resources or imposing continuing labor on others. It includes the space to read, think, create, explore interests, form beliefs, revise oneself, and live without being constantly converted into a task.

The point of this layer is defensive. If a person is learning, thinking, writing, creating, or experimenting within their own time and accessible resources, the public does not need to approve the purpose. The community’s role is not to decide whether the activity is valuable enough. Its role is to refrain from unnecessary intrusion and to maintain the shared conditions that make private freedom possible.

This is where Aletheia differs from theories that treat all development as something to be planned. The second layer protects development precisely when it does not require organized public support.

### 4.3 Public operation needs

The third layer includes the maintenance of shared systems: public health, water, energy, sanitation, emergency response, courts, basic education, infrastructure, environmental protection, and other conditions without which the first two layers cannot reliably exist.

This layer is instrumental. Public operation is not sacred in itself. Its authority exists to protect life, safety, freedom, and shared conditions. It must not transform itself into a privileged class, a permanent command structure, or a justification for absorbing more and more of private life.

### 4.4 Externally collaborative development needs

The fourth layer concerns forms of development that require resources beyond what an individual can reasonably provide alone: laboratories, expensive equipment, specialized materials, long-term funding, cross-regional coordination, or sustained collaboration from others. These needs may be valuable, but they do not automatically have the same priority as survival and basic freedom.

The fourth layer is where support, selection, and fairness become difficult. A person may have a genuine project, invention, research aim, artistic plan, or social experiment that requires collective assistance. The community may support it, especially where the project plausibly contributes to shared knowledge, capability, or future public needs. But the request must disclose costs, alternatives, expected benefits, affected parties, and limits. Support is not a blank check, and talent is not a general right to command resources.

### 4.5 Consumption needs

The fifth layer includes private consumption, luxury enjoyment, status display, comfort, entertainment, and market preferences. These needs are not morally worthless. Aletheia does not condemn pleasure, convenience, beauty, leisure, or invention motivated by laziness. Many useful innovations begin as someone’s desire to avoid repetitive effort or to make life easier.

The point is priority. Consumption needs may not invade the first four layers. They do not justify depriving others of survival, basic freedom, public operation, or fair access to collaborative development. Market demand can reveal preferences; it cannot by itself define human worth or public necessity.

## 5. Need attribution and public responsibility

One of Aletheia’s central moves is the principle of need attribution:

> The first responsibility belongs to the subject to whom the need belongs, not necessarily to the person who first expresses it.

This distinction prevents two opposite injustices.

The first injustice is privatizing public needs. A sick patient may be the person through whom the need for medical care appears, but the need for a medical system belongs to the public as a vulnerable community. Doctors, nurses, children, elderly persons, future persons, and healthy persons all inhabit a condition in which illness, injury, birth, disability, and aging are possible. Medical care is therefore not merely the private demand of the currently sick. It is a public need revealed in a particular case.

The second injustice is publicizing private desire. A person may want special treatment, luxury, emotional service, admiration, obedience, or exemption from ordinary limits. The fact that the person strongly feels or expresses the desire does not make it public. Others may voluntarily respond, but they are not automatically responsible for satisfying it.

Aletheia therefore distinguishes private needs, organizational needs, role-system needs, and public needs. Private needs belong primarily to the individual whose life purpose they serve. Organizational needs belong to the organization or task system that creates them. Public needs belong to the community when they are conditions of shared life or common vulnerability. Many events contain more than one layer and must be separated before responsibility is assigned.

This also changes the meaning of public responsibility. Public responsibility does not mean that every member performs the same task. It means that the community may not deny that the need belongs to it. Once a need is recognized as public, the community must establish some combination of financing, organization, training, resource supply, maintenance, oversight, and repair. Only after these public and organizational responsibilities are specified can one ask whether particular persons have limited, fair, and reviewable obligations to contribute.

## 6. Authority, factual power, and institutional boundaries

Aletheia rejects the idea that a need, a role, or a capacity automatically generates authority. Authority must be authorized. It must have an object, scope, duration, record, appeal path, and exit condition.

This applies to public institutions, professional roles, families, organizations, and experts. A doctor may have authority to diagnose and treat within professional and legal limits, but not to own the patient’s life. A teacher may guide learning, but not define the child’s ultimate purpose. A parent may care for a child, but parental responsibility is not ownership. A public official may administer a program, but the program is not the official’s domain. A talented leader may persuade others, but persuasion does not become permanent rule.

The framework also recognizes factual power. Someone may lack formal authority yet control the agenda, information, budget, data, infrastructure, evaluation, or access to remedies. Such control can shape other people’s choices as effectively as formal command. Aletheia therefore asks not only “who signed the order?” but also “who made alternatives invisible, who controlled the records, who set the price, who defined the metric, who could block appeal, and who benefited from the dependency?”

At the same time, the framework does not treat all influence as domination. Good advice, expertise, leadership, charisma, trust, and voluntary following are real and valuable. Some people are wiser in particular matters. Some people organize better. Some arguments deserve more weight. The danger is not excellence. The danger is the conversion of excellence into unreviewable authority, cross-domain control, or a permanent position from which others lose the power to choose whom they trust.

The institutional goal is not to make strong persons weak. It is to prevent strength from becoming ownership over others. The weak person need not possess a complete independent theory before having rights. They must retain the power to choose whom to believe, to accept an interpretation, to change that choice, to exit, and to seek another explanation. They may also bear reasonable consequences for choices they understand and accept. But their lack of certainty cannot be used as a reason to remove their subjecthood.

## 7. Labor, recognition, and the distribution of efficiency

Labor is one of the central sites where institutions forget the person. Aletheia therefore insists that laborers are not merely carriers of tasks or producers of outputs. They are subjects of experience, judgment, risk, time, and knowledge.

This is especially important for forms of work that are repetitive, intimate, invisible, or priced low by markets: care work, cleaning, maintenance, logistics, repair, food work, sanitation, elder care, disability support, and many forms of administrative coordination. Such labor often becomes visible only when it fails. When it succeeds, it disappears into the background and is treated as natural. Aletheia rejects this invisibility. Market price may evaluate exchange under particular conditions; it may not decide the dignity of the laborer or the social reality of the work.

Workers should have a recognized voice in the design of work. They should be able to describe unsafe procedures, hidden time costs, emotional burdens, unrealistic metrics, ineffective tools, and better methods. Where risks are controlled and affected persons are protected, workers should be allowed to test improvements with witnesses, records, and review. A system that ignores the knowledge of those who perform the work is not merely unfair; it is epistemically damaged.

The same point applies to efficiency gains. When a new tool, organization method, or technical process saves time, the saved time is rarely produced by management alone. It draws on workers’ cooperation, tacit knowledge, experiments, errors, and social infrastructure. Efficiency gains should therefore not default to unilateral organizational capture in the form of unpaid intensification, hidden overtime, reduced staffing with unchanged burden, or new surveillance. Their distribution should be negotiated and, where public systems are involved, publicly justified. Priority should be given to shorter working time, rest and recovery, safer conditions, better pay, improved service quality, and broader public benefit.

Aletheia does not yet specify a formula for this distribution. It only sets a boundary: efficiency may not be used to erase the laboring subject who helped produce it.

## 8. Innovation and public return

Aletheia distinguishes the origin of an innovation from its later public status. An invention may begin in externally supported research, in private convenience, in market demand, in laziness, in play, in frustration, or in a desire for beauty. The origin does not by itself determine whether the innovation later becomes publicly important.

The pathway can be stated simply:

> individual or collaborative development; or private convenience and market demand  
> → invention, knowledge, technology, or organizational innovation  
> → wider adoption  
> → public necessity  
> → public recognition through legitimate procedure  
> → return into life, freedom, or public operation layers  
> → limited and proportionate recognition for creators and contributors

This pathway matters because public need is not fixed forever. What once seemed optional can become a basic condition of life. Literacy, sanitation, vaccines, electricity, internet access, assistive technologies, data infrastructure, and emergency systems all show how social dependence can change. Aletheia therefore treats public responsibility as historically revisable. The public layer is not a closed list.

But public return has two dangers. If the public later depends on an innovation, the community may be tempted to seize it without acknowledging creators and contributors. Conversely, creators or firms may claim permanent private control over something that has become necessary for shared life. Aletheia rejects both extremes. Public necessity can justify public access, public support, public licensing, or public production, but it should not erase authorship, contribution, fair return, or the social knowledge that made the innovation possible. The difficult problem is to design time-limited, proportionate, and non-dominating forms of return.

## 9. Conflict, scarcity, and revisability

Aletheia does not promise to abolish conflict. A framework that claimed to satisfy every need without cost would either be false or coercive. Needs conflict. Resources are limited. Time cannot be multiplied at will. Public systems fail. People misjudge themselves and others. Experts disagree. Majorities can be wrong. Strong leaders can be useful and dangerous. Weak persons can make mistakes and still remain persons.

The institutional duty is therefore not to make conflict disappear. It is to make the structure of conflict visible. Any serious institutional decision should state its aim, evidence, affected parties, expected cost, opportunity cost, alternatives, distribution of burden, compensation, appeal path, review date, and conditions for suspension or reversal. A decision that cannot survive this disclosure should not be allowed to hide behind the language of necessity.

Where conflict cannot be resolved, Aletheia gives priority to avoiding irreversible harm. This includes harm that cannot be exited, repaired, compensated, or later reopened; harm that permanently destroys a person’s bodily safety, basic freedom, evidence, voice, or ability to challenge power; and harm that expands domination even while claiming to solve a public problem.

This principle is not a ban on all risk. Shared life involves risk. But it is a burden of justification: the more irreversible the harm, the stronger the evidence, participation, alternatives, safeguards, and repair mechanisms must be.

## 10. What this paper does not claim

This paper does not claim that Project Aletheia is a completed political system. It does not claim that its principles have been accepted by a public community, that its mechanisms have passed field trials, or that existing law already contains its distinctions. It does not propose to replace current institutions by immediate implementation.

It also does not claim that every public need creates equal tasks for every person. Public responsibility is not the same as uniform labor duty. A recognized public need first creates a responsibility for the public to organize, finance, maintain, and supervise a response. Only then may a community discuss limited and fair forms of individual contribution, and such contribution must remain bounded by capacity, safety, time, exemption, appeal, and recovery.

Nor does the paper claim that markets, expertise, leadership, family care, professions, or organizations are worthless. It asks that each remain within its proper boundary. Markets may coordinate exchange, but not define human value. Experts may clarify facts, but not own final purposes. Leaders may persuade, but not become permanent rulers. Families may care, but not possess. Organizations may coordinate labor, but not absorb the person’s time and identity. Public institutions may protect common conditions, but not define happiness.

## 11. Research agenda

The next stage of Aletheia should not be a leap from philosophy to implementation. It should be a sequence of tests designed to make the framework vulnerable to criticism.

First, the conceptual distinctions must be tested against hard cases: medical triage, elder care, disability support, emergency response, education, public infrastructure, reproductive responsibility, labor transition, and technological innovation. In each case, the framework should ask whether it correctly distinguishes private need, public need, organizational need, role-system need, execution responsibility, and authorization.

Second, the framework must be compared with existing institutional baselines. What problem does the existing arrangement already solve? What does it solve better than Aletheia’s proposal? What new cost would Aletheia introduce? Which costs are ethical costs, administrative costs, fiscal costs, coordination costs, or transition costs? A framework that refuses this comparison remains rhetoric.

Third, possible failure modes must be named in advance. Basic guarantees may become bureaucratic dependency. Public responsibility may become unlimited claim-making. Contribution records may become hidden status rankings. Expert review may become gatekeeping. Participation procedures may become delay tactics. Institutional boundaries may become excuses for non-response. A serious design must say where it stops.

Fourth, empirical tests must begin with low-risk scenarios. Project Aletheia has already begun preparing a baseline study of a forty-year-old ordinary enterprise worker in Shanghai who voluntarily leaves a job and attempts to transition into elder care work. That case is not evidence that Aletheia works. It is a way to identify the existing costs of transition: training cost, food and lodging during transition, wage uncertainty, social insurance continuity, certification incentives, hiring conditions, and the difference between job advertisements and actual contracts. The test is useful precisely because it may show that current systems already solve some problems, that Aletheia’s proposals are too costly, or that the real bottleneck lies elsewhere.

Fifth, high-risk domains should remain at the level of legal, ethical, and anonymous scenario review. Reproductive responsibility, child welfare, and family formation are examples where real-world experimentation would be ethically unacceptable before conceptual and procedural safeguards are far stronger.

## 12. Conclusion

Aletheia is an attempt to restore the person to the center of institutional thought without denying the need for common life. It begins with a simple refusal: no person should have to be managed, bound, or coerced in order to survive. From that refusal, it does not infer a society without responsibility. It infers a stricter account of responsibility.

Needs must be expressed, interpreted, attributed, and justified at the right level. Public needs must not be abandoned to the individuals through whom they become visible. Private desires must not be disguised as public necessity. Authority must not arise automatically from need, ability, expertise, majority, office, or emergency. Labor must not disappear behind price. Innovation must be able to return to the public without erasing creators. Strong persons may lead, persuade, and contribute, but not convert strength into ownership over others. Weak persons may lack complete certainty, but they do not lose the right to choose, revise, exit, and be heard.

The framework remains incomplete. Its value lies not in offering a finished blueprint, but in providing a disciplined way to ask what institutions are allowed to do in the name of human life. If institutions exist for persons, then every institution must be able to answer not only what it can accomplish, but where it must stop.
